\begin{abstract}
Training-free style transfer with a frozen text-to-image transformer conflates
two useful outcomes: preserving a reference composition while changing its
subject, and transferring only the reference medium to a new composition. We
formulate them as endpoints of a single \emph{style-locality} axis and control
both with \textbf{Test-Time Score Distillation (TSD)}. TSD evaluates a
same-state contrast between reference-conditioned and reference-free frozen
velocities, converts it into a fixed-magnitude rms-normalized latent update,
and interleaves that update with the model's ordinary reverse steps. This gives
an empirical \emph{regularization substitution}: in our single-sample
test-time regime, the frozen reverse trajectory repeatedly re-anchors the
edited latent, while an added learned fake-score term suppresses structured
media rather than improving stability. The number of reverse steps then acts
as a locality control: more steps preserve reference composition for
mask-free semantic style editing; fewer steps dissolve composition for global
style transfer. We evaluate style fidelity, subject fidelity, and composition
transfer separately using CSD, CLIP-T, and layout leak. Across 40 references,
TSD improves CSD from 0.267 to 0.719 in the composition-preserving setting and
from 0.247 to 0.615 in the composition-dissolving setting, winning all 40
paired styles in both cases, with a modest CLIP-T decrease of about 0.02. A
five-style, four-prompt extension yields 20/20 additional paired wins. TSD
trains no weights, but remains an optimization-based test-time method.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\textbf{Style transfer on a frozen generator is treated as one task, but
users want two.} Given a content prompt and a reference image, a frozen
text-to-image transformer can render the prompt's subject ``in the style of''
the reference. In practice two very different outcomes are desirable and are
conflated under one name. In one, an artist wants the reference's
\emph{scene} preserved --- its composition, its arrangement --- with only the
subject re-imagined in the reference's medium; this is \textbf{Semantic Style
Editing}. In the other, the artist wants only the reference's \emph{look}
--- its medium, texture, and palette --- carried onto a freshly composed
scene, with the reference's specific layout thrown away; this is
\textbf{Global Style Transfer}. Conflating them yields methods that silently
do one and fail the other.

\textbf{These two outcomes are the endpoints of a single controllable axis,
which we call style locality.} Style locality asks \emph{how much of the
reference is transferred}: at the local end, everything is transferred
(medium and composition), giving semantic editing; at the global end, only
the transferable, position-free part (medium, palette, texture statistics) is
transferred, giving global transfer. Framing the problem as one axis, rather
than two disjoint tasks, is what lets a single operator serve both --- and
lets a user dial between them.

\textbf{We drive this axis with one frozen-model operator, derived rather
than engineered.} Recent distribution-matching distillation \citep{yin2024dmd}
separates its update into a \emph{spear} (a classifier-free-guidance-
augmentation term that sharpens toward the conditioning) and a \emph{shield}
(a distribution-matching term, requiring a separately trained fake-score
network, that keeps samples on the data manifold) \citep{liu2025decoupled}.
We find that for \emph{test-time, single-sample} generation over a multi-step
frozen reverse process, the ordinary inference dynamics provide sufficient
regularization in our experiments, while adding a fake-score term suppresses
structured style. Removing that auxiliary network leaves a pure
frozen-teacher update, which we call \textbf{Test-Time Score Distillation
(TSD)}.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A reframing.} Training-free style transfer is a single
\emph{style-locality} axis whose two ends (Semantic Style Editing, Global
Style Transfer) are both first-class tasks; we serve both with one operator
and a single free knob (reverse-step count).
\item \textbf{An empirically tested regularization substitution.} We
interleave bounded latent updates with the frozen reverse trajectory and show
that an auxiliary fake-score network is unnecessary in the evaluated regime;
adding one erases structured media rather than improving stability.
\item \textbf{A separable-axis evaluation protocol.} We evaluate style
fidelity (CSD), subject fidelity (CLIP-T), and composition transfer
(layout-leak) as three \emph{separable} axes, complemented by a task-aware
six-dimensional Agent audit, and show that a single style number rewards
subject-destroying over-stylization --- a failure only the joint reading
exposes. (We also tested, and honestly reject, a
generator-native single-forward style metric read from the spear; \S~4.)
\item \textbf{A fair, strong baseline and a decisive result.} Against the
same frozen model \emph{directly instructed} to edit the image with its
strongest style-transfer prompt, TSD reaches \textbf{2.5--2.7$\times$ its
CSD} at matched subject fidelity across all 40 styles (with half the
variance), and --- unlike the native edit --- can dissolve composition while
keeping medium.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Distillation and test-time score guidance.}
Distribution-matching distillation matches a student generator to a teacher
through real-minus-fake score differences, with later variants improving the
objective and separating a distribution-matching \emph{shield} from a
CFG-augmentation \emph{spear}
\citep{yin2024dmd,yin2024dmd2,liu2025decoupled}. This decomposition connects
to diffusion and score models' noise-conditional vector fields
\citep{ho2020ddpm,song2021sde}: conditional contrasts yield classifier or
classifier-free guidance, score distillation, and text-guided editing
\citep{dhariwal2021diffusion,ho2022cfg,poole2023dreamfusion,hertz2023dds,
nam2024cds,chinchuthakun2025lusd}.
Other test-time methods steer frozen samplers with external losses, perturbed
attention, or annealed conditions
\citep{bansal2023universal,ahn2024pag,hong2024seg,sadat2024cads}. TSD instead
specializes the spear to one test-time sample: it optimizes only the current
latent and empirically substitutes the frozen reverse trajectory for a
separately trained fake-score network.

\paragraph{Reference-conditioned style transfer and frozen-model editing.}
Classical style transfer matches feature statistics
\citep{gatys2016style,huang2017adain}; diffusion methods extend this idea
through exemplar conditioning, learned style interfaces, attention sharing,
or sampling-time transport
\citep{yang2023paint,sohn2023styledrop,ye2023ipadapter,hertz2024stylealigned,
chung2024styleinjection,deng2024zstar,wang2024instantstyle,xu2025stylessp,
he2025semantix,rout2025rbmodulation}. Related editing systems control cross-attention or reuse
frozen diffusion features \citep{hertz2023prompt,tumanyan2023plugplay}, while
rectified-flow methods develop inversion and direct trajectory editing
\citep{rout2025rfinversion,kulikov2025flowedit}. TSD belongs to the
optimization-based, training-free-of-weights family, but its signal is the
native same-state reference-conditioning contrast rather than replaced
attention features, a trained adapter, or an explicit mask. Accordingly, we
do not target exact motif replication through attention key/value
manipulation; we target the transferable medium, palette, and texture
statistics controlled by the style-locality axis.

\section{Method}

Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} summarizes the operator derived in this section as
an alternating cycle of task-direction distillation, a bounded latent update,
and frozen-prior regularization.

$g_t=v_{\mathrm{target}}-v_{\mathrm{base}}$ supplies the task direction (a

\subsection{Velocity Differences Are Score Differences}

A rectified-flow transformer \citep{esser2024sd3} predicts a velocity
$v_\theta(z_t,t,c)$ at latent
$z_t$, time $t$, conditioning $c$. For the flow-matching schedule
\citep{lipman2023flow,liu2023rectified} the velocity is an affine function of
the conditional score,
$s_\theta(z_t,t,c)=-\,(z_t-(1-\sigma)\hat x_0)/\sigma^2$, so a
\emph{difference} of velocities under two conditionings equals, up to one
positive schedule factor, a difference of conditional scores:
\begin{multline}
v_\theta(z,c_1)-v_\theta(z,c_2)\;\propto\;\nabla_z\log p_t(z\mid c_1)\\[2pt]
{}-\nabla_z\log p_t(z\mid c_2)\;=\;\nabla_z\log\frac{p_t(z\mid c_1)}{p_t(z\mid c_2)}.
\label{eq:vscore}
\end{multline}
Every velocity difference the frozen model gives us is therefore the
gradient of a log-density ratio we may ascend (attract) or descend (repel).
This is the whole toolbox: no auxiliary network, only frozen forward passes.

\subsection{The Spear: CFG-Augmentation as the Style Driver}

Decoupled distribution matching \citep{liu2025decoupled} writes its
distillation gradient as
\begin{equation}
\nabla \;\propto\; \underbrace{(s_{\text{cond}}-s_{\text{fake}})}_{\text{shield
(distribution match)}}\;+\;\underbrace{(\alpha-1)\,(s_{\text{cond}}-s_{\text{uncond}})}_{\text{spear
(CFG augmentation)}}.
\label{eq:spearshield}
\end{equation}
The spear is a \emph{conditional contrast} evaluated at the sample's own
state, structurally the image-conditioned analogue of classifier-free
guidance \citep{ho2022cfg}; it needs only the frozen teacher. We instantiate
the style spear with two frozen velocities: $v_{\text{with}}$, conditioned on
the reference image (the style evidence), and $v_{\text{without}}$,
conditioned on the style \emph{text} alone. The style guidance is
\begin{equation}
g \;=\; v_{\text{with}} - v_{\text{without}} \;\propto\; \nabla_z \log
\frac{p_t(z\mid \text{reference})}{p_t(z\mid \text{style text})},
\label{eq:stylespear}
\end{equation}
the extra style evidence the reference \emph{image} supplies beyond what
words can.

\subsection{The Regularization Substitution: The Frozen Reverse Process
\emph{Is} the Shield}

\textbf{The shield stabilizes repeated generator updates during distillation
training.} In DMD, the fake-score term discourages the learned generator from
drifting away from the teacher distribution. Our setting differs: we optimize
one latent while retaining the frozen model's ordinary $T$-step reverse
trajectory. Each bounded TSD update is followed by a standard reverse step,
which repeatedly reintroduces the frozen model prior. We therefore test an
empirical regularization substitution: whether these inference dynamics make a
separately learned fake-score regularizer unnecessary in the single-sample
regime.

\textbf{The trained shield is unnecessary in the evaluated regime.} If the
conditional contrast caused unstable drift, increasing its pre-normalization
scale should expose artifacts. Instead, scales from 4 to 20 produce
near-identical outputs because rms normalization removes that scale, and no
collapse is observed across the tested trajectories. This does not establish
formal distribution matching; it shows that an auxiliary fake-score network
is not needed for the evaluated test-time procedure.

\textbf{The trained shield is harmful in our implementation.} Adding a real
fake-score critic removes visible structured media: for example, mosaic
tesserae revert toward a naturalistic rendering. Scheduled fake-score updates
also fail to improve three styles with remaining headroom. These observations
support using the frozen reverse process as the only regularizing dynamics,
without claiming that the same conclusion holds for every model or sampler.

Dropping the shield and keeping the spear gives \textbf{Test-time Score
Distillation (TSD)}.

\subsection{The RMS-Normalized Pseudo-Target: a Fixed-Magnitude
Extrapolation}

We apply the spear as a pseudo-target in $x_0$-space rather than as a raw
gradient. With $\hat x_0 = z - \sigma\,v_{\text{without}}$ the current
denoised estimate, we form a detached target that is the estimate nudged
along the \emph{unit} style direction,
\begin{equation}
x_0^{\text{tgt}} = \sg\!\big(\hat x_0 + \eta\,\widehat{g}\big), \qquad
\widehat{g}=\rmsnorm(g),
\label{eq:pseudotarget}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L = \tfrac12\big\lVert \hat x_0 - x_0^{\text{tgt}}\big\rVert^2,
\label{eq:pseudotargetloss}
\end{equation}
so the latent update is exactly $\eta\,\widehat{g}$: a \textbf{fixed-magnitude
step} of size $\eta$ along the style direction, applied every inner
iteration. Two consequences follow. First, because rms-normalization removes
the overall scale of $g$, the guidance weight $\alpha$ and any linear
coefficient are \textbf{washed out} --- only the \emph{direction} of $g$ and
the step size $\eta$ affect the output. Second, a fixed-magnitude step is an
\emph{extrapolation past} the weak fixed point $v_{\text{with}}=v_{\text{without}}$,
whereas minimizing $\lVert g\rVert^2$ would \emph{interpolate to} that fixed
point and saturate early; the former transfers structured texture the latter
misses while preserving content.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{TSD inference (one style transfer)}
\label{alg:tsd}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE frozen model, reference image, prompt; reverse steps $T$, inner
iterations $K$, step size $\eta$
\STATE start from a noisy latent $z_T$
\FOR{reverse step $t = T$ \TO $1$}
  \FOR{$k = 1$ \TO $K$}
    \STATE read the model's velocity \emph{with} the reference
    ($v_{\text{with}}$) and \emph{without} it ($v_{\text{without}}$)
    \STATE $\mathrm{spear}\gets$ direction of
    $(v_{\text{with}}-v_{\text{without}})$ \hfill// the style signal
    \STATE nudge $z_t$ one small step $\eta$ along the spear
    \hfill// change the latent only
  \ENDFOR
  \STATE take one ordinary frozen reverse step to get $z_{t-1}$
  \hfill// the only regularizer
\ENDFOR
\RETURN the image decoded from $z_0$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Style Locality: One Operator, Two Modes, One Free Knob}

\textbf{Semantic Style Editing is the full spear on the reference.} With
$g=v_{\text{with}}-v_{\text{without}}$ attracting to the whole reference ---
its medium \emph{and} its composition --- the prompt's subject is rendered in
the reference's medium \emph{within the reference's composition}. Subject
preservation is emergent: no mask, no segmentation, no region blending.

\textbf{Global Style Transfer is the same spear with fewer reverse steps.}
Reducing the reverse-step count under-determines the sample's structure, so
the reference's specific composition never solidifies, while the per-step
style spear still imprints the reference's medium and palette. Composition
dissolves; medium remains. The step count is a \emph{free, continuous} knob
along the style-locality axis --- from many steps (local,
composition-preserving) to few steps (global, composition-dissolving).

\subsection{Why Velocity Space Stops at the Transferable Part (Analysis)}

\textbf{The frozen-velocity spear transfers the position-free part of style,
not the exact motif layout --- which is exactly what the axis should
control.} Reproducing a reference's \emph{specific} tessellation or leading
requires attention-level key/value manipulation, which we exclude. To
characterize the boundary, we probe a taboo-clean, \emph{position-only}
construction: we compute the reference velocity with the reference tokens'
positional identifiers scrambled, isolating the reference's appearance from
its layout (this touches no attention statistics --- only input positions
consumed by the frozen model). Guided by this layout-free velocity, global
transfer succeeds cleanly on \emph{medium-dominant} styles (relief, halftone,
stained-glass leading --- the medium survives position scrambling) but
collapses the subject on \emph{figure-dominant painterly} styles (portrait
mosaics, oil portraits), where medium and composition are entangled. The
reverse-step knob is the robust instrument; the layout-free probe explains
\emph{why} certain references dissolve cleanly and others do not, and it
supplies the generator-native metric of \S~4.

\section{Evaluation Protocol and Perceptual Diagnostics}

\textbf{We measure three separable axes, because style transfer is not one
number.} Style fidelity (is the medium transferred?), subject fidelity (is
the prompt's subject present?), and composition transfer (is the reference's
layout kept or dissolved?) can move independently, and a single metric hides
failures. We report \textbf{CSD} (Contrastive Style Descriptor cosine
similarity to the reference --- style fidelity, designed to be
content-invariant, though as we show below it is gameable at \emph{extreme}
over-stylization) \citep{somepalli2024csd}, \textbf{CLIP-T} (image--text
alignment to the content prompt --- subject fidelity) \citep{radford2021clip},
and a \textbf{layout-leak} measure (correlation of off-diagonal patch
self-similarity --- composition transfer). Layout-leak cleanly separates the
two modes in our study: high for semantic editing (composition kept), low for
global transfer (composition dissolved).

\textbf{CSD alone is misleading, which is why we pair it with CLIP-T.} In the
semantic grid, pushing the spear harder monotonically raises CSD but past a
point \emph{destroys the subject}: the highest-CSD configuration (CSD
$\approx 0.82$) has CLIP-T collapsed to $\approx 0.21$ and layout-leak
$\approx 0.82$ --- the panda is being over-painted into pure texture. The
right operating point balances CSD and CLIP-T rather than maximizing CSD;
reporting both makes over-stylization visible instead of rewarding it. This is
a boundary artifact, not our operating regime: we verify in \S~5 that our
\emph{benchmark} CSD advantage is not driven by it --- the gap survives at
matched layout-leak.

\textbf{A task-aware Agent benchmark resolves the same trade-off perceptually.}
For each of 40 references, a primary VLM receives the reference and two
anonymous, randomly ordered candidates for the matched task. It scores six
independent 1--5 axes: medium/technique, texture/mark-making, palette/tone,
subject fidelity, task locality, and visual quality. Locality means
\emph{composition preservation} for Semantic Style Editing and
\emph{composition independence} for Global Style Transfer. The primary judge
covers all 80 paired task--style cells; a secondary model audits 68 cells, and
failed API responses are not imputed. Prompts, raw responses, bootstrap
intervals, and judge controls are provided in the supplement.

\textbf{The Agent results localize the gain instead of hiding the cost.}
TSD is preferred in 39/40 Semantic and 37/40 Global pairs. Relative to the
matched native edit, its mean gains on medium, texture, and palette are
$+2.5$--$+2.9$ points, while subject fidelity is $0.6$ points lower. Semantic
composition preservation improves by $+3.45$ points. In Global transfer,
however, composition independence is $1.63$ points lower than the native
new-composition edit: TSD transfers far more style but retains more reference
layout, consistent with layout-leak. The two Agents agree on the preferred
method in all 68 shared cells; their correlations are $0.96$ on the three
style axes and $0.93$ on task locality.

\textbf{Public-editor audit.} On a fixed five-style audit against
Qwen-Image-Edit-2511 \citep{wu2025qwenimage} and FireRed-Image-Edit-1.1
\citep{firered2026edit} (Table~\ref{tab:external-audit}), TSD leads the
reference-style axes: Semantic medium/texture/palette average \textbf{4.60}
versus $4.00$ (Qwen), $3.60$ (FireRed), and $2.53$ (native); Global
\textbf{4.87} versus $4.13$, $2.27$, and $2.47$. The primary Agent selects TSD
as the best-style candidate in $3/5$ Semantic and $5/5$ Global cells, and a
full Gemini-3.5 Flash re-audit agrees on $80\%$ of cells per task. The public
editors retain stronger literal subject fidelity ($5.00$ versus TSD's $3.80$ in
Semantic) --- the same style$\leftrightarrow$subject trade-off seen against the
native edit --- while the native path alone keeps best composition independence
in Global. Per-axis cross-Agent correlations are in the supplement.

\textbf{A generator-native style metric, explored and rejected (honest
negative result).} External style metrics such as CSD and DINO
\citep{oquab2024dinov2} judge with a
\emph{separate} pretrained network; it is tempting to read style straight
from the generator by re-evaluating the spear on the output,
$\text{SRSD}(x,R)=\lVert v_{\text{with}}(x;R)-v_{\text{without}}(x)\rVert_{\text{rms}}$,
expecting a small residual when $x$ already carries $R$'s style. We tested
this ($n=20$, four configurations $\times$ five styles) and reject it. Its
correlation with CSD is \textbf{positive, not negative} (Pearson $+0.79$,
Spearman $+0.88$), and the correlation is carried almost entirely by
over-stylized samples whose \emph{content has collapsed} into a
near-verbatim copy of the reference --- exactly the samples CSD wrongly
rewards. Controlling for spear strength, the residual correlation vanishes
($r=0.20$, n.s.), and scale-correction does not rescue it. The spear
magnitude marks \emph{content collapse / composition copying}, not style
faithfulness; flipping its sign would reward the pathology. This is the same
style--composition entanglement that bounds velocity-space transfer (\S~3.6):
a frozen-velocity scalar cannot isolate pure style. We therefore keep the
three external, separable axes and report this as a cautionary null.

\section{Experiments}

\paragraph{Setup.} A single frozen text-to-image rectified-flow transformer
serves as both conditional and unconditional predictor (one model, shared
runtime); all results are training-free of weights. Unless noted, generations
use seed 65 and the prompt ``a panda eating bamboo'' on a 40-reference style
set spanning mosaic, relief, oil, halftone pop-art, and stained-glass media.
We report CSD, CLIP-T, layout-leak, and the task-aware Agent audit.

\subsection{The Style-Locality Axis and the Parameter Study}

\textbf{The live knobs are step count / inner iterations, step size $\eta$,
and per-token normalization; guidance weight is inert.} Consistent with
\S~3.4, sweeping the guidance weight leaves the output unchanged
(rms-normalization washes it out); we therefore grid the knobs that matter.
For \textbf{Global Style Transfer} (few-step regime), CSD rises with step
size, peaking at \emph{num-steps 8, $\eta=0.12$} (CSD $\approx
0.63$--$0.64$), above the default 8-step anchor (CSD 0.53), while layout-leak
stays low ($\approx 0.45$, composition dissolved). For \textbf{Semantic Style
Editing} (25-step regime), CSD rises with inner iterations and step size but
over-stylizes at the top; the balanced optimum is \emph{iters 5,
$\eta=0.08$} (CSD 0.74, CLIP-T 0.28), versus the canonical anchor (CSD 0.70,
CLIP-T 0.285). The complete grids for both normalization choices, including
all CSD, CLIP-T, and layout-leak values, are reported in the supplement.

\subsection{Full Benchmark Against the Frozen Model's Native Edit}

\textbf{Baseline design (a fair, strong baseline, not a straw man).} The most
honest baseline is the \emph{same} frozen model \emph{directly instructed} to
edit the reference with text --- its native image+text edit. We first verify
that this is a genuinely different mechanism: the frozen reverse process in
our pipeline does not itself consume the edit image (the reference enters
only through the spear), so ``our operator with zero spear'' is prompt-only
text-to-image, not a native edit. We therefore run the model's own native
edit pipeline at its documented best settings. Because that checkpoint
exposes no system-prompt channel, we fold a strong expert style-transfer
instruction into the prompt (and a negative prompt that deliberately does
\emph{not} penalize flat media, to avoid disadvantaging pop-art and
stained-glass). This strong prompting substantially improves the baseline
(mean CSD from $0.24\rightarrow0.36$ for the composition-preserving
instruction, $0.03\rightarrow0.29$ for the new-composition instruction), so
the comparison is against the baseline's best effort.

\caption{\textbf{The medium transfers across content, not just one subject.}
Five media-diverse styles (rows) are each applied to two very different content
prompts --- a panda and a lighthouse. The leftmost column is the shared style

\label{fig:dense-grid}

\textbf{Result (40 styles).} Even at its best, the native edit is dominated
on style fidelity by a wide margin across references (Table~\ref{tab:benchmark}). TSD
reaches CSD \textbf{0.72 $\pm$ 0.08} (semantic) and \textbf{0.61 $\pm$ 0.09}
(global) versus \textbf{0.27 $\pm$ 0.16} and \textbf{0.25 $\pm$ 0.16} for the
native edit --- a \textbf{2.5--2.7$\times$ style-fidelity gain} (2.69$\times$
semantic, 2.48$\times$ global). The most robust form of the claim is not the
mean: \textbf{ours wins CSD on every one of the 40 styles, in both pairings
(40/40 semantic, 40/40 global)} --- despite the native edit's large cross-style
dispersion (whose lower tail overlaps ours), it never wins a single style
(Figure~\ref{fig:mainresults}a). A
spot-check on the native edit's worst style (a neon-anime reference, its CSD
$\approx 0$) shows why: the native edit defaults to a generic realistic
panda that ignores the reference medium entirely, so its near-zero CSD is a
genuine style-transfer failure, not a scoring artifact. Two things sharpen
the result. First, the gap is at \emph{matched or slightly lower} subject
fidelity: the native edit keeps CLIP-T marginally higher ($\approx 0.315$ vs.
0.30) because it stylizes less --- an honest style$\leftrightarrow$subject
trade-off, visible on the CSD--CLIP-T frontier (Figure~\ref{fig:mainresults}b).
Second, the native edit's CSD dispersion across references is \emph{twice}
ours (SD 0.16 vs. 0.08): it is not only weaker but far less consistent across
styles, succeeding on genuinely
relief-like media (\eg, bronze) and reverting to a \emph{generic}
engraving/line-art look elsewhere, missing the specific
tesserae/impasto/halftone/leading that TSD transfers consistently
(Figure~\ref{fig:dense-grid}). Finally,
when instructed to change composition, the native edit \emph{loses the
medium} (its new-composition variant scores CSD 0.25, below its own
composition-preserving 0.27), whereas TSD's step-knob dissolves composition
while \emph{keeping} the medium (global CSD 0.61) --- the native edit cannot
separate style from layout, which is exactly the global-transfer end of the
axis.

\textbf{Is the style-fidelity gap real style, or just more
reference-copying?} Because CSD can be gamed by near-verbatim reference copies
at extreme over-stylization (\S~4), and our outputs carry higher layout-leak
than the native edit, a skeptic could ascribe the CSD gap to copying rather
than style. It is not. We lead the pure-style claim with \emph{Global Style
Transfer}, whose whole point is to \emph{dissolve} the reference's
composition: its layout-leak (0.41) is close to the native edit's (0.30), yet
it still wins CSD 0.61 vs.\ 0.25 --- a 2.5$\times$ gap at comparable
composition-copying. More directly, we restrict to the 14 styles where our
global output copies \emph{no more} layout than the native edit (layout-leak
$\le$ its mean$+1\sigma = 0.36$) and find ours still wins CSD \textbf{14/14}
(0.61 vs.\ 0.24); within the global grid the CSD--leak correlation is weak
($+0.29$). The gap survives at matched layout-leak, so it is style fidelity,
not composition-copying.

CLIP-T is computed against the literal content prompt (``a panda eating
bamboo''), uniformly across all four methods, so the comparison is fair;
scoring against the fuller style-template prompt shifts every method up by a
near-constant $\approx 0.03$ and preserves the ordering. CSD and layout-leak
are prompt-independent.

\textbf{The CSD advantage is robust across content prompts.} To confirm the
win is not an artifact of the single benchmark prompt, we re-ran a
media-diverse 5-style subset (mosaic, bronze, oil, pop-art, stained-glass)
across four additional content prompts (a seaside castle, a cat on a
windowsill, a sunflower field, an aged fisherman). Ours wins CSD on every
style$\times$prompt cell --- \textbf{20/20 in both pairings, zero flips} ---
with per-prompt ratios of 1.55--2.31$\times$ (semantic) and 1.65--2.20$\times$
(global). The multiplier on this subset is $\approx 1.9$--$2\times$, not the
2.5--2.7$\times$ headline: the headline is the full 40-style figure, whereas
this deliberately media-diverse subset is harder and its own single-prompt
anchor already sits at $\approx 2\times$. The style$\leftrightarrow$subject
trade-off is prompt-dependent --- near-matched CLIP-T on subject-light
prompts, but on the \emph{portrait} prompt our CLIP-T falls further (gap up to
$\approx 0.08$) as the spear over-stylizes the face --- the same honest
trade-off, more visible where subject identity is strong.

\subsection{Ablations}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{reverse_shield/reverse_shield.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{The frozen reverse step is the regularizer.} Scaling the frozen
scheduler displacement $z\!\leftarrow\!z+\rho\,(\mathrm{step}(z)-z)$ by
$\rho\in\{1,0.5,0\}$ at a fixed task update (two representative styles). At
$\rho=1$ (the original reverse process) the stylization is clean; at $\rho=0$ the
scheduler is still evaluated but its displacement is withheld, so the
fixed-magnitude task updates alone drive the latent off-distribution into noise;
$\rho=0.5$ interpolates. The collapse is style-independent (all five styles in
the supplement).}
\label{fig:reverseshield}
\end{figure}

\textbf{The frozen reverse step is load-bearing.} We scale the scheduler
displacement $z\!\leftarrow\!z+\rho\,(\mathrm{step}(z)-z)$ by $\rho\in\{1,0.5,0\}$
at a fixed task update (Figure~\ref{fig:reverseshield}). At $\rho=0$ the model is
still evaluated but its displacement withheld, so the task updates alone drive
the latent and every style drifts off-distribution into noise; $\rho=0.5$
partially degrades. This is not merely disabling the sampler --- the task
optimization is unchanged --- so it is the reverse process, not the task update,
that keeps the optimized latent coherent. \textbf{A trained shield is
unnecessary and harmful.} Conversely,
adding a trained fake-score shield (the literal DMD term) degrades style:
structured media are pulled back toward naturalism (mosaic tesserae lost). The
frozen reverse process alone is the better regularizer in the training-free
regime. \textbf{Spear direction vs.\ magnitude.} The fixed-magnitude
rms-normalized step (extrapolation) transfers texture that magnitude-minimizing
objectives (interpolation to the fixed point) miss, at equal content
preservation. \textbf{Guidance weight.} Sweeping $\alpha$ leaves outputs
unchanged, confirming rms-normalization removes scale --- the direction is the
signal.

\subsection{Evaluation Robustness and Failure Modes}

\textbf{The Agent audit is judge-robust.} To reduce dependence on a single model
family, a third VLM (a local Gemma-4-family checkpoint) re-scored the 95
calibration outputs. It agrees moderately with both Gemini judges (Pearson
$0.59$--$0.69$; at least $82\%$ of ratings fall within one point) and, decisively,
passes the pre-specified wrong-reference control: its mean style score drops from
$4.21$ for the correct reference to $1.33$ for a reproducibly assigned wrong
reference, and it scores the correct reference higher on $89.5\%$ of outputs. The
audit therefore reads reference-specific style rather than assigning uniformly
high scores; it remains a judge-robustness test, not a human study.

\textbf{Where TSD gives up subject alignment.} The trade-off concentrates on
subject-heavy references, as the portrait case above shows; on already-graphic
references the medium is instead near-saturated, so the gain is neutral rather
than negative. In Global transfer the medium survives while composition
dissolves, yet the reference layout is retained more than in the native
new-composition edit --- the honest cost of transferring more style, visible as
the higher layout-leak in Table~\ref{tab:benchmark}. None of these is a scoring artifact; each is the same
style--subject trade-off the frontier is built to expose, which is why we report
paired axes rather than a single style score.

\textbf{Cost.} TSD is training-free of weights but optimization-based at
inference: the reported settings run five inner latent updates per reverse step
(Semantic) or a few-step schedule (Global), adding compute over a plain sample
while training nothing. Memory is dominated by the frozen backbone, so a single
generation fits in about $24$\,GB and completes in roughly a minute on one GPU;
we optimize only the current latent and keep no auxiliary network.

\section{Conclusion}

Training-free style transfer on a frozen transformer is a single
\emph{style-locality} axis served by one operator: the CFG-augmentation spear of
decoupled distribution matching, applied as a fixed-magnitude rms-normalized
latent step. Its non-obvious ingredient is an empirical \emph{regularization
substitution}: interleaving bounded updates with the frozen reverse trajectory
removes any need for a learned fake-score network in the evaluated regime.
Across 40 styles this lifts mean CSD by 2.48--2.69$\times$ over the same model's
strongest native edit, at a modest subject-alignment cost --- with no training,
no auxiliary fake-score network, and no mask or segmentation.
